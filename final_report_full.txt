\begin{abstract}
This project implements an AI-powered mock interview platform designed to provide a realistic, structured, and repeatable interview-practice experience. The implementation is organized as a React and TypeScript client, an Express-based Node.js orchestration layer, and a Python analysis service responsible for audio, transcript, and vision-related processing. The delivered platform includes configuration and consent handling, preview-question generation, WebRTC-based live interviewing, supportive-mode hints and instant feedback, CV upload and candidate-brief extraction, incremental answer-audio ingestion, multimodal report generation, persistent report archival, history analytics, token-cost estimation, and a Docker-based multi-service deployment setup. Unlike a static chatbot prototype, the current project implements a complete interview lifecycle with explicit session state, backend guardrails, asynchronous artifact completion, and evidence-based feedback presentation. This report is based only on the source code and automated tests currently present in the project.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interviews play a critical role in early career development, as they are widely used to evaluate candidates' technical knowledge, communication skills, and overall suitability for a position. For students and recent graduates, effective interview preparation is therefore an important step toward improving employment opportunities and professional confidence. However, many candidates lack access to realistic, repeatable, and feedback-oriented practice environments \cite{hirevue2023,pymetrics2023}.

Traditional preparation methods, such as reviewing frequently asked questions or conducting informal mock interviews, can help candidates become familiar with interview formats, but they often provide only limited benefit. In most cases, these approaches do not offer structured, objective, or measurable feedback. As a result, candidates may struggle to assess the quality of their answers, the clarity of their communication, and their ability to respond effectively under pressure.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have created new opportunities for more interactive and adaptive interview preparation systems. AI-based tools can simulate interviewer behavior, generate context-aware questions, and evaluate candidate responses at scale \cite{openai2024gpt,russell2021ai}. However, many existing solutions primarily focus on question generation or general answer scoring, while offering limited insight into broader communication behaviors and overall interview performance \cite{fister2024ai,suen2024video}.

This project presents an AI-powered mock interview platform designed to provide a realistic, structured, and repeatable interview practice experience. The platform enables users to participate in voice-based interview sessions with an AI interviewer and receive evidence-based feedback on both response quality and communication patterns. By combining conversational AI with structured session management and performance evaluation, the system aims to make interview preparation more accessible, personalized, and effective for early-career candidates.

\subsection{Purpose}
The purpose of this project is to develop an interview practice platform that supports realistic simulation and meaningful performance evaluation. The system is intended to help users improve not only the content of their answers but also the way they communicate during an interview.

More specifically, the platform aims to provide a configurable interview environment based on role, difficulty, and company or industry context, while also generating evidence-based post-session feedback from multiple signals. In this way, the project goes beyond simple question-answer interaction and focuses on supporting measurable user improvement through structured practice.

\subsection{Scope}
The scope of the implemented platform includes the complete lifecycle of a mock interview session, from preparation to post-session reporting. Based on the current system design and project structure, the platform covers the following functionalities:
\begin{itemize}
\item session setup with required microphone and camera consent,
\item optional CV upload and candidate-specific briefing generation,
\item preview-question generation before the interview session,
\item real-time voice-based interview execution,
\item supportive and neutral interviewer modes,
\item incremental upload and processing of candidate answer audio,
\item transcript, audio, and visual analysis of interview performance,
\item report generation and archival of session artifacts,
\item history and trend views across previous sessions.
\end{itemize}

The current scope is limited to interview-session orchestration, multimodal analysis, and reporting functionalities. A persistent relational database and a full user-account management subsystem are not included in the same codebase. Instead, active session data is managed in memory, while completed report artifacts are archived in the server-side reports directory \cite{b2,b4}.

\section{Problem Definition}
Conventional interview preparation methods are often insufficient for candidates who need realistic speaking practice and objective feedback. Static question lists may help users review common interview topics, but they do not replicate live interaction, follow-up questioning, or time-constrained spoken responses. Similarly, informal mock interviews can provide useful impressions, yet they are difficult to standardize, repeat consistently, and evaluate objectively \cite{hirevue2023,pymetrics2023}.

Another important limitation is the lack of real-time support in many interview-preparation tools. In many cases, feedback is provided only after the interview has ended, which limits the candidate's ability to recognize weaknesses during the interaction itself. As a result, users may complete practice sessions without receiving timely guidance on how to adjust their response structure, speaking flow, or communication behavior while the interview is still in progress \cite{fister2024ai,suen2024video}.

This limitation becomes more visible in the context of Turkish interview practice. Tools and examples that work well for English-centered interview preparation are not always sufficient for Turkish-speaking candidates, especially when speech clarity, fluency, and role-specific communication need to be evaluated in Turkish. This creates a gap between generic AI assistance and the needs of users who require a Turkish-speaking interviewer, Turkish transcript handling, and feedback that reflects spoken performance rather than only text quality.

From a system-design perspective, the problem is not limited to generating interview questions. An effective interview-preparation platform must also manage interview flow, preserve conversational context across turns, adapt to different interview styles, and produce actionable evaluation based on multiple forms of evidence. These forms of evidence may include spoken responses, transcript content, delivery patterns, and visible engagement signals \cite{zhang2025llm,goodfellow2016deep}.

The problem addressed in this project is therefore the design and implementation of a structured AI-based interview system that can simulate a realistic interview process while also producing comprehensive and evidence-based feedback. To address this need, the implemented platform combines conversational AI with rule-based orchestration and multimodal post-session analysis. Rather than treating the interview as a single prompt-response exchange, the system models a complete session lifecycle that includes preparation, live interaction, evaluation, and review \cite{b1,b2,b3,b4}.

\section{Background Research, Similar Designs, and Engineering Principles}

\subsection{Background Research and Similar Designs}
Background research indicates that AI-supported interviewing systems are increasingly used to automate parts of candidate screening, structured interviewing, and interview preparation. Commercial examples such as HireVue and Pymetrics demonstrate that AI can be integrated into interview-related workflows for question delivery, response analysis, and candidate assessment at scale \cite{hirevue2023,pymetrics2023}. Academic and professional discussions also show that AI-enabled interviews raise important questions about realism, fairness, transparency, and user trust \cite{fister2024ai,luo2025ai,suen2024video,fabris2025algorithmic}.

Compared with such systems, the implemented project focuses on interview practice rather than employment decision-making. This distinction is important from both a technical and ethical perspective. The system is designed to help users improve through repeated practice, supportive guidance, and evidence-based post-session reporting, rather than to make automated hiring decisions. Nevertheless, similar-design research remains relevant because it highlights both the opportunities and risks of AI-mediated interview environments \cite{fister2024ai,raghavan2020mitigation}.

\subsection{Component Information}
The project combines several component classes commonly found in modern AI-enabled systems. The conversational layer relies on large language models for question generation, interviewer behavior, and narrative feedback support \cite{openai2024gpt,zhang2025llm}. The signal-analysis layer uses established machine-learning and deep-learning techniques for transcript, audio, and vision processing \cite{goodfellow2016deep,russell2021ai}. In addition, the web platform integrates browser media capture, WebRTC communication, and modular backend orchestration to support low-latency interactive use.

These component choices reflect practical engineering trade-offs. LLMs provide flexibility and naturalness in interaction, whereas deterministic backend guardrails improve control, consistency, and safety. Similarly, multimodal analysis broadens the evidence base for feedback, but also increases system complexity and introduces additional privacy and fairness considerations \cite{fabris2025algorithmic,luo2025ai}.

\subsection{Basic Engineering Principles}
Several basic engineering principles guide the implemented design. First, modularity is preserved by separating the client, orchestration backend, and analysis service into distinct layers. Second, robustness is improved through validation, fallback logic, duplicate suppression, and graceful degradation under partial-service failure. Third, maintainability is supported through explicit session lifecycle management and well-separated responsibilities across components. Finally, user-centered engineering considerations are reflected in supportive-mode guidance, consent enforcement, and evidence-oriented feedback presentation.

These principles are consistent with broader software-engineering and AI-system design practice, where systems are expected not only to function correctly, but also to remain understandable, extensible, and safe under realistic operating conditions \cite{russell2021ai,goodfellow2016deep}.

\section{System Overview and Solution Approach}
\subsection{System Perspective}
The system is implemented as a layered architecture with three major runtime zones. The first zone is a web client written in React 19, TypeScript, and Vite. The second zone is an Express server that manages validation, orchestration, persistence, routing, and OpenAI-facing communication. The third zone is a Python analysis service that performs audio, transcript, and vision-related computation. This makes the platform a full-stack application with both synchronous and asynchronous feedback paths. In addition to this logical separation, the repository includes a Docker Compose-based deployment structure that runs the client, server, and Python analysis service as distinct containers with health-checked startup ordering and shared report storage \cite{b6}.

\subsection{Solution Approach}
The implemented solution adopts a hybrid approach. Large language models are used to generate questions, maintain the conversational flow, and interpret several forms of feedback, while deterministic backend rules keep the interview process bounded and consistent \cite{openai2024gpt,zhang2025llm}. This combination is important because a purely prompt-driven flow can become unstable in longer sessions, whereas a fully rule-based system would not provide the flexibility needed for natural conversation, role-aware questioning, and mode-dependent interviewer behavior.

The system also separates interaction management from analysis. During the session, the platform focuses on realtime interviewing, supportive guidance when enabled, and artifact collection. After or near the end of the session, transcript, audio, and vision signals are analyzed through dedicated processing layers and then aggregated into a unified report. This separation improves modularity and makes it possible to extend one part of the platform without redesigning the entire workflow.

Another important aspect of the implementation is the use of fallback and degradation mechanisms across multiple layers. The system includes rule-based safeguards for invalid session states, consent enforcement before interview start, duplicate-aware handling of incremental audio uploads, transcript fallback when direct transcript evidence is incomplete, provisional audio reporting while final analysis is still pending, and graceful degradation when external analysis services become unavailable or time out. In the vision pipeline, MediaPipe-based detection is supported by an OpenCV fallback path so that the reporting flow can continue even when the preferred detector is not available. These mechanisms improve robustness by ensuring that the interview process and post-session reporting remain functional under partial-data and partial-service conditions.

\subsection{Product Functions}
The codebase currently implements the following product-level functions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Collect interview configuration fields including first name, last name, gender, role, company or industry, domain interest, difficulty, and interaction mode.
\item Enforce microphone and camera consent before the session can proceed.
\item Generate preview questions tailored to HR or technical interviews.
\item Conduct a live interview with an AI interviewer and display real-time audio and transcript updates during the session.
\item Provide supportive-mode hints and toast feedback during the interview.
\item Upload candidate answer audio incrementally while the interview is running.
\item Analyze transcript quality, answer relevance, audio delivery, and visual presentation.
\item Build a unified report containing scores, recommendations, transcript text, and analysis status flags.
\item Re-open previous reports and show cross-session trends in the history screen.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{User Workflow}
The live workflow begins at the setup screen. The setup stage constructs the initial interview configuration and validates it through a consent-aware form flow. The start action remains disabled until all mandatory fields are populated and both microphone and camera consent are granted. If the user uploads a PDF CV, the client reads the file, encodes it as Base64, and attaches it to the outgoing session request \cite{b1}. This allows the system to move beyond generic interviewing and prepare a more context-aware session.

The preview screen performs client-side device checks. Video testing is based on a local camera stream rendered inside a preview panel. Audio testing uses the browser audio-processing stack and an analyser configuration. The screen also requests three preview questions from the backend and exposes editable candidate-summary fields if a backend-generated summary is available.

The interview screen then opens the actual runtime path. It starts the live camera, starts browser-side vision sampling, creates the WebRTC connection for the interviewer session, maintains a rolling transcript, captures interviewer audio clips, and periodically flushes candidate answer audio every 2.5 seconds. During this stage, the platform behaves as a voice-based interview system rather than a text chatbot: the user interacts through speech, the interviewer responds in realtime, and supportive-mode interventions can be injected when that operating mode is active. When the user finishes the interview, the client sends the transcript and all candidate-answer audio artifacts to the backend and transitions to the feedback page with an optimistic placeholder report until asynchronous analysis becomes ready.

The feedback screen polls the backend report endpoint and updates the visible analysis status when transcript, audio, or vision artifacts become ready. The history screen lists archived sessions, retrieves metadata from stored reports, filters by mode and interview type, and visualizes cross-session score patterns with chart components.

\section{System Architecture and Technical Design}

\subsection{Architectural Overview}
The system follows a three-tier design: (i) a browser client responsible for configuration capture, device diagnostics, realtime interview interaction, and visualization; (ii) a Node.js/Express orchestration backend that manages the session lifecycle, policy checks, realtime signaling, and artifact persistence; and (iii) a Python analysis service that produces transcript, audio, and vision analysis artifacts. Persisted artifacts are stored on disk under \texttt{server/reports} to support report retrieval and cross-session history without a database.

\subsection{Technology Stack}
\textbf{Client.} The client is built with React (19.2.0), TypeScript (\textasciitilde5.9.3), and Vite (\textasciicircum7.2.4). UI composition uses Tailwind CSS (with PostCSS and \texttt{tailwindcss-animate}) and Radix UI primitives. Class composition and styling utilities include \texttt{clsx}, \texttt{class-variance-authority}, and \texttt{tailwind-merge}. For UX and visualization, the client uses Lucide React, Framer Motion, and Recharts. \\
\textbf{Backend.} The backend is a Node.js server using Express (4.19.2) with \texttt{cors} and \texttt{dotenv}. It uses \texttt{pdf-parse} for CV text extraction and \texttt{ffmpeg-static} to support audio conversion workflows. Automated checks follow Vitest and Supertest-based patterns. \\
\textbf{Python analysis service.} The Python service uses FastAPI and Uvicorn with Pydantic and \texttt{python-multipart}. Core analysis dependencies include PyTorch, Torchaudio, Transformers, Librosa, NumPy, SoundFile, OpenCV (headless), and MediaPipe. The stack also includes \texttt{qdrant-client[fastembed]} for vector search/embeddings, and \texttt{requests}/\texttt{matplotlib} for HTTP utilities and visualization/debug support \cite{goodfellow2016deep}. \\
\textbf{OpenAI services.} OpenAI is integrated as a runtime dependency: realtime interviewing is configured with \texttt{gpt-realtime-mini}; live input transcription uses \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-transcribe}. Preview question generation and several post-hoc narrative/report steps use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}. A Node-side transcript evaluation component uses \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, and candidate-audio transcription fallback uses \texttt{gpt-4o-transcribe} \cite{openai2024gpt}. \\
\textbf{Containerization and deployment.} The repository also includes Dockerfiles for the client, backend, and Python analysis service, together with a \texttt{docker-compose.yml} file that defines the multi-container runtime, shared report volume, service health checks, and environment-variable-based configuration \cite{b6}.

\subsection{Client Layer}
The client implements navigation using a route-key model rather than a dedicated routing library. A navigation snapshot is stored in browser history state, which preserves the active route, the current session configuration, and the latest report payload during back/forward navigation.
The Setup screen collects identity and interview configuration and enforces mandatory microphone and camera consent before proceeding. An optional CV PDF can be uploaded; the client reads the file and forwards the encoded payload as part of session configuration.
The Preview screen performs device diagnostics. For audio, it uses an \texttt{AnalyserNode} with \texttt{fftSize=256} and derives a normalized live level from average frequency-bin energy. For video, it requests a \texttt{1280$\times$720} stream and renders a live preview. The screen triggers backend-driven preview question generation and allows editing of candidate-brief text fields when CV-derived summaries are present.
The Interview screen connects to the realtime session, maintains transcript state, and periodically flushes incremental candidate answer audio uploads every 2.5\,s while active, with a final flush during session termination. It also integrates supportive-mode UI elements (hints and instant feedback toasts) when enabled. After the interview ends, it closes realtime/media resources and transitions to the feedback view with an optimistic placeholder report while asynchronous analysis completes.
The Feedback screen polls for report readiness and progressively renders transcript, audio, and vision artifacts. The History screen lists archived sessions and enables opening prior reports, with trend and distribution visualizations.

\subsection{Backend Layer}
The backend is an Express server initialized with CORS, JSON parsing, and a plain-text body parser that explicitly supports \texttt{application/sdp} for realtime signaling payloads. It also provides a backward-compatible \texttt{/log} endpoint for transient client RTC/debug logs and mounts the main session router.
The router exposes endpoints for session creation, session start, configuration updates, consent persistence, preview question generation, supportive-mode utilities (hints, instant feedback, and usage recording), realtime SDP offer/answer exchange, incremental answer ingestion, vision-frame ingestion, session finalization, report retrieval, archived report listing, and history insight computation.
Core coordination is implemented in \texttt{BackendOrchestrator}. Active sessions and reports are stored in in-memory repositories, while persistent artifacts are written through a filesystem-backed archive. Consent and start-state validation are enforced through \texttt{GuardrailsEngine} (microphone and camera consent are mandatory; invalid start states are rejected). During an active session, the backend maintains runtime structures for incremental candidate audios, saved audio file references, analyzed-audio bookkeeping, and a vision sub-state that aggregates detector-derived signals and counters.

\subsection{Python Analysis Layer}
The Python analysis layer is implemented as a standalone FastAPI service (served via Uvicorn) that provides HTTP endpoints for transcript, audio, and vision processing. Requests are modeled and validated with Pydantic, and \texttt{python-multipart} is used for file-compatible payload handling where required. The service relies on ML and signal-processing libraries including PyTorch, Torchaudio, Transformers, Librosa, NumPy, SoundFile, OpenCV (headless), and MediaPipe to compute structured metrics and scores from session artifacts. The project also includes \texttt{qdrant-client[fastembed]} to support embedding-based retrieval components used by analysis utilities. At session finalization, the Node.js backend invokes this service to produce finalized analysis outputs (e.g., transcript/audio/vision report artifacts) that are persisted into the session's archive directory and later consumed by the UI and history computations.

\subsection{External Interfaces}
The system exposes four primary interface surfaces. First, the browser interface uses MediaDevices and Web Audio APIs for capture and diagnostics, plus History State for lightweight routing and state persistence. Second, a REST interface connects the client to the Express backend for session lifecycle operations, supportive-mode utilities, incremental artifact ingestion, and report retrieval. Third, a WebRTC-based interface connects the client, backend SDP exchange, and the OpenAI realtime service for low-latency interviewing, including a data channel for model events. Fourth, an HTTP bridge connects the Node.js backend to the Python FastAPI analysis service to trigger transcript/audio/vision analysis and to materialize finalized report artifacts that are later served to the client. This separation ensures that capture, orchestration, external model calls, and deterministic analysis remain decoupled while still producing a unified report view.

\subsection{Realtime Communication Path}
Realtime interviewing is implemented with WebRTC. The client creates an \texttt{RTCPeerConnection} configured with a public STUN server, adds a receive-only audio transceiver for interviewer audio, opens a data channel (\texttt{oai-events}) for model events, and injects the local microphone track into the peer connection. The backend exchanges SDP via \texttt{/realtime/offer} and configures the realtime session, including server-side voice activity detection (VAD) with threshold-based turn detection and automatic response creation.
The project contains two VAD configurations: a default server policy (\texttt{threshold=0.95}, \texttt{prefix\_padding\_ms=300}, \texttt{silence\_duration\_ms=1500}) and a client-side \texttt{session.update} that sets \texttt{silence\_duration\_ms=700}. Transcript events are normalized into stable transcript entries with role, timestamp, and source/model metadata, and lightweight fingerprinting is applied to reduce duplication.

\subsection{Persistence and Report Artifacts}
Session artifacts are persisted under \texttt{server/reports/S-*/}. The archive includes:
\texttt{session\_config.json} (session metadata), \texttt{transcript.txt} (plain transcript text),
\texttt{transcript\_report.json} (structured transcript analysis),
\texttt{audio\_segments.json} (incremental audio model outputs/segments),
\texttt{audio\_report.json} (final audio interpretation),
\texttt{vision\_frames.json} (sampled vision frames/metrics),
and \texttt{vision\_report.json} (final vision report).
These artifacts are loaded by the backend to serve report views and to compute cross-session history insights.

\section{Mode-Driven Interviewer Behavior and Personalization}
Question generation and supportive features are implemented through a dedicated AI service layer (\texttt{AIServiceGateway}) and structured prompt templates (\texttt{PromptTemplates}). The implementation explicitly distinguishes between HR and technical interviews. For preview generation, both tracks request exactly three questions. HR preview generation incorporates a curated HR question bank loaded from \texttt{hr\_question\_bank.json} and instructs the model to maintain theme diversity across behavioral categories such as motivation, teamwork, conflict management, feedback, and time management. Technical preview generation conditions on role, company/industry, domain interest, and difficulty, and requests three domain-constrained technical questions with explicit diversity constraints (e.g., avoid paraphrases and avoid over-focusing on a single project), optionally grounding questions with CV-derived evidence when available.

The live interviewer is governed by explicit session instructions assembled by \texttt{PromptTemplates.sessionInstructions(cfg)} and injected into the realtime session via the session builder. These instructions enforce Turkish-only output and prescribe an interview macro-structure (OPENING $\rightarrow$ QUESTION LOOP $\rightarrow$ CLOSING). They also include anti-repetition and diversity constraints, such as limiting how long the interviewer may stay on the same project/example and requiring thematic switching across the interview. Mode selection affects interpersonal style: in Supportive mode the interviewer may provide brief guidance when the candidate is stuck, whereas in Neutral mode the interviewer remains more formal and less directive. In addition, content-domain rules are specialized by interview type (HR vs.\ Technical), so interviewer behavior is driven along two orthogonal dimensions: (i) interpersonal style (Supportive vs.\ Neutral) and (ii) content domain (HR vs.\ Technical).

CV-driven personalization is implemented as a first-class feature in the filesystem-backed archive layer (\texttt{FileReportArchive.saveCvFile}). When a PDF CV is uploaded, the backend persists the original PDF under the session archive, extracts text using a PDF parsing utility, and produces a structured CV representation (\texttt{cv.json}). The backend may additionally produce an optional Turkish translation file (\texttt{cv\_tr.txt}) using a dedicated translation helper (\texttt{cv\_translator.py}, default model \texttt{Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-tr}). From the structured CV, the system derives a compact candidate brief (\texttt{candidate\_brief.json}) containing both technical and HR-oriented summaries and highlight lists (e.g., education, experience, projects, skills, HR experience highlights, and inferred behavioral focus areas). This brief is reused in preview prompting and is also incorporated into live session instructions, with explicit guidance to treat CV statements as hypotheses to be validated through the candidate's spoken answers.

Supportive mode adds two runtime services invoked by the client. First, live hint generation: when the interviewer finishes a question, the client sends the question text to \texttt{POST /session/:id/supportive/hints} and receives up to three concise hints for structuring the next answer. Second, live feedback: after the client detects completion of a candidate answer (from transcript role transitions), it sends the previous question and answer to \texttt{POST /session/:id/supportive/feedback} and receives one short coaching message formatted for toast presentation. These supportive calls use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} with structured JSON responses, and token usage for these calls is recorded via the backend usage endpoint. The implementation also includes guards to skip hints for introductory questions and to avoid generating feedback for extremely short or trivial answers.

\section{Evidence-Based Feedback Pipeline}
The implemented system runs three complementary analysis tracks over interview data: transcript analysis, audio analysis, and vision analysis. These tracks are integrated through a common persistence and reporting layer (filesystem-backed session archives under \texttt{server/reports}) rather than being isolated experiments.

\subsection{Transcript Analysis}
Transcript feedback is produced in two complementary stages. First, at session finalization the Node.js backend generates an initial report via a transcript evaluator component. Second, a dedicated Python transcript analyzer produces the structured transcript artifact (\texttt{transcript\_report.json}) that powers per-question scoring, categorization, and higher-level recommendation grouping in the UI and history views.

The Python pipeline starts by normalizing the raw transcript into stable question--answer (QA) units and applying lightweight cleanup to reduce fragmentation and remove non-substantive turns. In particular, the pipeline detects and excludes setup/meta interactions (e.g., greetings, ``hazırsanız başlayalım'', or closing pleasantries) so that scoring focuses on interview substance rather than dialogue management.

For each interviewer question, the analyzer assigns a \texttt{questionType} through a dedicated resolution layer. The resolver first applies rule-based meta detection; if a question is identified as setup/closing, it is labeled \texttt{meta} and excluded from scoring. Otherwise, semantic retrieval is performed using Qdrant over a small, seeded set of labeled example questions. The resolver retrieves the nearest examples (filtered by interview type), aggregates similarity scores per label to estimate confidence, and selects the best label when confidence is sufficient. If retrieval confidence is low, the system falls back to an LLM-based classifier to resolve ambiguous cases. The supported labels include \texttt{self\_presentation}, \texttt{motivation}, \texttt{behavioral}, \texttt{experience}, \texttt{technical\_knowledge}, \texttt{technical\_experience}, and \texttt{problem\_solving} (with \texttt{meta} excluded from scoring).

After question typing, the Python analyzer computes per-QA rule-based metrics in addition to any LLM-produced narrative interpretation. These metrics include relevance and clarity signals (e.g., lexical overlap and surface-level structure features), depth and example/evidence strength, and technical accuracy when applicable. Each QA entry is also annotated with visibility and scoring flags (e.g., \texttt{visibleInReport}, \texttt{excludedFromOverall}) to ensure that the overall score reflects interview substance rather than meta-dialogue.

Finally, recommendation generation is explicitly structured. The transcript artifact groups suggestions into three user-facing horizons: \emph{Bir Sonraki Mülakatta} (immediately actionable tactics), \emph{Performans Geliştirme} (mid-term interview technique improvement), and \emph{Çalışma Planı} (topics to study/practice).

\subsection{Audio Analysis}
Audio analysis is implemented in the FastAPI-served Python service and is executed incrementally during the interview as candidate answer audios are uploaded. Each uploaded answer is converted to WAV and analyzed as an individual segment with \texttt{finalize\_report=false}, producing evolving per-segment outputs in \texttt{audio\_segments.json}. At session finalization, the system generates the final user-facing audio report (\texttt{audio\_report.json}) without re-processing the full set of raw audio files.

During startup, the audio module initializes two main models: a Turkish clarity/intelligibility model (\texttt{mpoyraz/wav2vec2-xls-r-300m-cv7-turkish}) and an emotion classifier from the SUPERB benchmark (\texttt{superb/wav2vec2-base-superb-er}). Each answer is loaded at \SI{16}{kHz} and analyzed across three signal families:
(1) emotion distribution via \texttt{AutoModelForAudioClassification},
(2) clarity via CTC confidence using a Wav2Vec2 model,
and (3) speaking behavior features estimated with Librosa (e.g., non-mute intervals via \texttt{librosa.effects.split} with \texttt{top\_db=25} and onset/peak statistics used to approximate speech rate).

The final audio report combines deterministic scores with an LLM-interpreted narrative layer. It includes an overall audio score, dominant/secondary emotion tendencies, tone distribution summaries, a \texttt{speechSummary} field, and practical recommendations (e.g., next-interview tactics and medium-term development). The pipeline also supports provisional (non-final) audio artifacts so the feedback page can correctly represent ``pending'' behavior until \texttt{audio\_report.json} is completed.

\subsection{Vision Analysis}
Vision analysis is split into (i) a browser-side sampler and overlay stabilizer and (ii) a server-side analyzer that accumulates session-level visual signals.
On the client, the vision sampling layer captures a JPEG frame approximately every \SI{900}{ms}, encodes it as Base64, and uploads it to the backend via \texttt{POST /session/:id/vision/frame}. To stabilize the user-facing overlay, bounding boxes are smoothed using a blending factor of 0.55, and the last valid box may be retained for up to \SI{650}{ms} when detection temporarily drops.

On the server, each ingested frame is analyzed by a Python vision module. The implementation prefers MediaPipe face detection and falls back to OpenCV Haar cascades when MediaPipe is unavailable or does not yield a face. The analyzer returns detector diagnostics and signals such as face count, bounding box coordinates, and eye-visibility proxies. The backend maintains a runtime vision sub-state that accumulates sampled frames, detected-face ratios, average face area and center offset, head-movement estimates, warning/danger counters, and whether supportive overlay guidance was shown.

At report finalization, these stored visual signals are summarized into scores such as face presence, focus/attention stability, centering, steadiness, and tension-related indicators (e.g., visual tension, attention risk, movement risk, and eye tension). A finalized vision report artifact (\texttt{vision\_report.json}) is produced for the UI, and the system degrades gracefully when MediaPipe is unavailable because diagnostics and fallback reasons are explicitly recorded.

\subsection{Aggregation, Reporting, and History}
The backend composes analysis outputs into a single report view (served via \texttt{GET /session/:id/report}) rather than exposing independent analysis endpoints. The report response is assembled by loading persisted feedback artifacts from the filesystem archive and merging them with any in-memory report state when available. Concretely, the response includes transcript text (\texttt{transcript.txt}), transcript analysis (\texttt{transcript\_report.json}), raw incremental audio outputs (\texttt{audio\_segments.json}), the finalized audio report (\texttt{audio\_report.json}), raw vision runtime payload (\texttt{vision\_frames.json}), and the finalized vision report (\texttt{vision\_report.json}). It also includes score metadata (a score schema for UI rendering) and analysis-status flags derived from which artifacts exist. Operational metadata such as token usage and estimated cost is included when present in the in-memory session report; for purely archived reconstructions these fields may be \texttt{null}.

A filesystem-backed archive persists session artifacts under a session-specific directory \texttt{server/reports/S-*/}. These files are consumed by the History page to list sessions (via \texttt{GET /reports}) and compute cross-session insights (via \texttt{GET /reports/history-insights}). The history insight logic loads recent reports, groups transcript QA evaluations by \texttt{questionType}, and derives trend metrics only for categories that are present across the selected recent reports, avoiding trends computed from sparse labels.

\subsection{Test Assessment Summary}
The verification results indicate a successful overall test outcome. All 23 requirement-oriented test cases and all 43 automated smoke tests were completed successfully, and no failed test cases were observed during the evaluated baseline.

Although no critical failures were encountered, manual verification and execution-based observation revealed several practical areas for improvement. The most notable issues were related to asynchronous report readiness behavior and temporary delays caused by external analysis-service dependencies. These issues did not prevent successful session completion, but they indicate opportunities to improve system robustness and user-perceived responsiveness.

Based on the current test outcomes, the most relevant directions for future improvement are:
\begin{itemize}
\item replacing polling-based report updates with event-driven or push-based completion signaling,
\item expanding browser-level end-to-end automated testing for live media workflows,
\item strengthening persistence and recovery mechanisms for longer-running sessions, and
\item reducing operational dependence on external services through additional caching, fallback, or recovery strategies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Current Engineering Risks and Testing Limitations}
Although the current verification baseline is successful, several engineering limitations remain relevant. First, the automated suite is primarily smoke-oriented rather than a full browser-media end-to-end validation suite. Second, external AI and analysis services are abstracted in automated tests, even though they remain operational dependencies in realistic deployments. Third, report readiness is driven by asynchronous artifact completion, and the current user-facing flow still relies on polling rather than push-based completion signaling. Fourth, while the project includes a Dockerized multi-service runtime for more reproducible deployment, broader production-scale concerns such as orchestration hardening, secret management, and long-running operational monitoring remain outside the present implementation scope. Finally, while core runtime behavior was validated through both automated and manual testing, persistence and recovery characteristics under long-running or failure-prone production conditions have not yet been evaluated as extensively as the main functional workflow.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Delivered Technical Capabilities}
Based on the implemented project and the verified system behavior, the project currently delivers a substantial set of technical capabilities. The system supports configurable HR and technical interview scenarios, together with both supportive and neutral interaction modes. It provides Turkish-language interviewer behavior, incorporates CV-based personalization into interview preparation, and enables live interview interaction through a WebRTC-based communication flow. In addition, the platform collects candidate transcripts and answer audio, performs transcript-, audio-, and vision-based analysis, stores generated session outputs for later access, and supports history-oriented report retrieval together with aggregated insight generation \cite{b1,b2,b3,b4}.

\subsection{Architectural Strengths}
One of the strongest properties of the system is its explicit coordination of multiple heterogeneous components within a single interview lifecycle. Browser-side media capture, server-side orchestration, AI-driven prompting, analysis services, and report persistence are separated into clear functional modules while still contributing to one coherent end-user workflow. This modular structure improves maintainability and makes the implementation easier to reason about.

The architecture also reflects several practical engineering strengths. Session guardrails are enforced before interview start, candidate audio can be uploaded incrementally instead of only at the end of the session, duplicate answer-audio submissions are filtered, and report generation can progress even when some analysis artifacts arrive later than others. In addition, fallback and degradation paths are implemented so that the system can continue operating in a controlled manner when some external analysis services are unavailable.

\subsection{Observed Limitations}
Despite the breadth of implemented functionality, the current system still exhibits several practical engineering limitations. During active operation, parts of the runtime continue to rely on in-memory state, which constrains persistence and recovery robustness under more demanding deployment conditions. In addition, some analysis artifacts are produced asynchronously, so the feedback workflow currently depends on polling rather than push-based completion signaling. The client-side history mechanism is also primarily application-state-driven instead of URL-oriented, which reduces flexibility for deep-linking and direct navigation.

From an evaluation perspective, the project is supported by both automated smoke testing and manual verification. However, this evidence should be interpreted as strong functional validation rather than exhaustive production-level assurance. In particular, full browser-media end-to-end behavior, live external-service interaction, long-running persistence guarantees, and recovery under sustained real-world load would still benefit from broader deployment-scale testing. These limitations do not indicate conceptual weaknesses in the architecture; rather, they reflect the current engineering boundaries of the implemented system.

\section{Contemporary Issues Related to AI-Based Interview Systems}
AI-based interviewing systems are currently discussed not only as technical tools but also as socio-technical systems with significant ethical and practical implications. One major issue is fairness. When AI models are used to interpret speech, language, or visual signals, there is a risk that different users may be treated unequally due to bias in training data, modeling assumptions, or evaluation criteria \cite{raghavan2020mitigation,binns2018fairness,buolamwini2018gender,buyl2022fairness}. In the context of interview-related systems, such concerns become especially important because communication style, visual presentation, and linguistic variation can differ substantially across users.

A second contemporary issue is trust and transparency. Users may benefit from AI-generated interview guidance, but they may also be uncertain about how feedback is produced or how much confidence should be placed in automated interpretations. Recent literature on AI-enabled interviews emphasizes that perceived fairness, trust, and explainability strongly affect user acceptance of such systems \cite{luo2025ai,suen2024video}. For this reason, AI-assisted interview platforms should be designed so that generated feedback is interpreted as decision support or practice assistance, rather than unquestionable judgment.

A third issue concerns privacy and responsible data use. Systems that process transcript, audio, and visual data necessarily handle sensitive personal information. This creates obligations related to informed consent, secure storage, limited retention, and clear communication about how user data is processed. In addition, the broader literature on algorithmic hiring warns that even supportive AI systems can shape user behavior and perceptions in ways that require ethical reflection \cite{fister2024ai,fabris2025algorithmic}.

The implemented project does not function as an automated hiring decision system; instead, it is intended as a practice and feedback platform. Nevertheless, these contemporary issues remain relevant because they influence how such a system should be engineered, evaluated, and communicated to users. Accordingly, the project should be understood within the broader context of fairness-aware, privacy-aware, and user-centered AI system design.

\section{Impact Analysis}
The implemented system has implications across global, economic, environmental, and societal dimensions. By combining artificial intelligence, web technologies, and multimodal analysis, it provides a scalable and accessible approach to interview preparation. At the same time, its deployment raises practical concerns related to fairness, privacy, and responsible use \cite{fister2024ai,luo2025ai,fabris2025algorithmic}. This section discusses both the positive contributions and the broader implications of the project.

\subsection{Global Impact}
The platform reflects a broader global shift toward AI-assisted professional preparation and digitally mediated career development. Because the system is web-based and does not depend on in-person interview coaching, it can potentially be used by candidates in different geographic locations with fewer logistical constraints. In this sense, the project supports wider access to structured interview practice and aligns with the global trend toward remote, technology-supported learning and assessment environments \cite{hirevue2023,pymetrics2023}.

\subsection{Economic Impact}
The system can improve economic efficiency by reducing dependence on costly interview-preparation resources such as private coaching services or paid mock-interview platforms. Because the platform automates routine practice sessions and is accessible online, users can prepare more flexibly and at lower cost. From an operational perspective, the use of automated interviewing and feedback generation also reduces reliance on human interviewers for repetitive training scenarios, which supports scalability. More broadly, the system may contribute to improved job readiness and reduce the time required for effective interview preparation.

\subsection{Environmental Impact}
As a web-based platform, the system supports remote preparation and reduces reliance on physical materials and face-to-face practice environments. This can decrease paper consumption, transportation requirements, and the environmental costs associated with travel. Although the platform depends on digital infrastructure and computational resources, its overall footprint may still remain lower than that of traditional preparation approaches that require physical attendance or printed resources. For this reason, the system can be regarded as a comparatively resource-efficient alternative for many interview-practice scenarios.

\subsection{Societal Impact}
The system has the potential to create positive societal value by making structured interview preparation more accessible to a wider range of users. Candidates from different educational, social, and economic backgrounds may benefit from guided practice without depending on professional networks or expensive services. In addition, the platform supports the development of communication, self-expression, and interview-performance skills through repeated practice and personalized feedback.

At the same time, the societal use of such a platform requires careful attention to ethical concerns. Because the system processes audio, transcript, and visual data, explicit consent, secure handling of user data, and clear privacy safeguards are necessary. Similarly, the use of AI-based evaluation introduces risks related to bias, fairness, and over-reliance on automated judgment \cite{raghavan2020mitigation,binns2018fairness,buolamwini2018gender,buyl2022fairness}. These concerns do not eliminate the value of the system, but they do require responsible design, transparent usage, and careful interpretation of generated feedback.

\section{Conclusion}
This project delivers a well-designed AI-assisted mock interview platform rather than a simple conversational interface. The implemented system integrates interview configuration, prompt-driven interviewer behavior, supportive and neutral interaction modes, WebRTC-based live interviewing, CV-aware personalization, multimodal evidence collection, report generation, session archival, and history-oriented analytics within a single end-to-end workflow.

From a technical perspective, the strongest aspect of the platform is its orchestration architecture. The client, backend, and analysis subsystems are separated clearly enough to remain maintainable, while still being integrated closely enough to provide a unified interview and feedback experience. The repository also demonstrates a practical containerized deployment approach through Docker-based service separation for the client, server, and Python analysis layer. The verification results presented in this report further indicate that the core user flow, API behavior, guardrail logic, fallback handling, and analysis-readiness behavior are functioning as intended in the current project state.

Accordingly, the project can be concluded to represent a full-stack mock interview platform that combines mode-aware interviewer behavior, practical system orchestration, and evidence-oriented feedback generation. At the same time, as discussed in the evaluation sections, broader production-scale validation would further strengthen confidence in long-running persistence, realtime robustness, and live external-service behavior.
